Error metrics: bias, MAE, RMSE and R²

  • Show All Code
  • Hide All Code

How far off are the predictions? The standard metrics, what each one rewards, and where they break.

Question answered: how far are the predictions from the actual values, on average?

We start with California Housing (median house value per district, in units of 100,000 USD), a target with a strong signal where these metrics behave as textbooks describe. At the end, we apply them to pure premium and watch them break.

The four basic metrics

With errors \(e_i = \hat y_i - y_i\) over \(n\) test cases:

Metric Formula Unit Question it answers
Bias (mean error) \(\frac{1}{n}\sum e_i\) target Is the model systematically too high or too low?
MAE (mean absolute error) \(\frac{1}{n}\sum \lvert e_i \rvert\) target How large is a typical error?
RMSE (root mean squared error) \(\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum e_i^2}\) target How large are errors, with big ones weighted more?
R² (coefficient of determination) \(1 - \frac{\sum e_i^2}{\sum (y_i - \bar y)^2}\) none What share of the variance does the model explain?

With weights \(w_i\) (as for pure premium, weighted by exposure), each sum becomes a weighted sum and \(n\) becomes \(\sum w_i\).

pd.DataFrame({m: regression_metrics(y, h[m]) for m in ("Null", "GLM", "GBM")}).round(3)

Reading the table:

  • Bias is close to zero for all models. That’s expected on data like the training data: least squares fits reproduce the mean. Bias becomes interesting once the data shifts: a growing bias is one of the first signs of drift (covered on a later page).
  • The GBM’s typical error (MAE) is 29,724 USD, against 52,970 USD for the GLM and 91,005 USD for always predicting the mean.
  • RMSE is always at least as large as MAE. The gap grows with the share of large errors: squaring makes a single error of 3 count as much as nine errors of 1.
  • R² compares the squared error with that of the Null model: the GBM removes 85% of the Null model’s squared error, the GLM 59%.

What MAE and RMSE reward

MAE and RMSE don’t just weigh errors differently, they reward different predictions:

  • Squared error is minimized by the mean. A model trained or selected on RMSE learns the conditional mean of the target.
  • Absolute error is minimized by the median. A model selected on MAE learns the conditional median.

For a symmetric target the two coincide. For a skewed target, such as house prices, claim costs or incomes, they don’t. Scoring every constant prediction shows this:

Code 
c = np.linspace(0.5, 4.0, 200)
mae = [np.mean(np.abs(y - v)) for v in c]
rmse = [np.sqrt(np.mean((y - v) ** 2)) for v in c]

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.8))
for values, name, color in [(mae, "MAE", METRIC_COLORS[0]), (rmse, "RMSE", METRIC_COLORS[1])]:
    ax.plot(c, values, color=color, label=name)
    ax.annotate(name, (c[-1], values[-1]), xytext=(4, 0), textcoords="offset points",
                va="center", fontsize=9, color=INK_SECONDARY)
for stat, value, side in [("median", y.median(), -1), ("mean", y.mean(), 1)]:
    ax.axvline(value, color=INK_SECONDARY, linewidth=1)
    ax.annotate(f"{stat} {value:.2f}", (value, 1), xycoords=("data", "axes fraction"),
                xytext=(4 * side, -4), textcoords="offset points", va="top",
                ha="left" if side > 0 else "right", fontsize=9, color=INK_SECONDARY)
ax.set(xlabel="Constant prediction (100k USD)", ylabel="Error (100k USD)")
ax.legend(loc="lower right")

The MAE curve bottoms out at the median, the RMSE curve at the mean. So choosing a metric is choosing what the model should predict. For pricing, where the average cost must be covered, the mean is the right target and squared-error-type metrics are the right family. MAE answers a different question.

WarningMAPE and other relative errors

The mean absolute percentage error, \(\frac{1}{n}\sum \lvert e_i / y_i \rvert\), is popular because it is unit-free. It is undefined when \(y_i = 0\), explodes when \(y_i\) is small, and penalizes over-prediction more than under-prediction, which pushes models to predict too low. Use it only for targets well away from zero, and never for pure premium.

Looking at the errors

A single number hides where a model errs. Two plots do most of the work:

  • actual vs predicted: points should scatter evenly around the diagonal,
  • residuals vs predicted: residuals should scatter evenly around zero, with no trend, no fan shape and no visible structure.
Code 
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 2, figsize=(9, 8.5))
for col, m in enumerate(("GLM", "GBM")):
    plot_actual_vs_predicted(y, h[m], ax=axes[0, col], title=m)
    plot_residuals(y, h[m], ax=axes[1, col])
fig.tight_layout()

Three things stand out, none of which the metrics table shows:

  1. A horizontal line at 5.0 (a diagonal stripe in the residual plots). 4.6% of districts have a value of exactly 5.0, because values were capped when the data was collected. The true values are higher, so the errors there are partly an artifact of the data, not of the model.
  2. The GLM’s residuals trend downward. It under-predicts cheap districts and over-predicts expensive ones: the relationship isn’t linear, and a linear model can’t bend to follow it. It also predicts outside the possible range for 1.3% of districts (above the cap or below zero).
  3. The GBM’s residuals are tighter and centered on zero across the whole range, consistent with its lower RMSE, but they widen for expensive districts. The errors are larger where values are larger, which is common for prices and amounts.

Where these metrics break: pure premium

Now apply the same metrics to pure premium, weighted by exposure. We add one more ‘model’: predicting 0 for every policy.

p = pure_premium_predictions()
preds = {m: p[m] for m in ("Null", "GLM", "GBM")} | {"Always 0": np.zeros(len(p))}
pd.DataFrame({m: regression_metrics(p["y"], v, p["w"]) for m, v in preds.items()}).round(4)

The results are strange:

  • ‘Always 0’ has the best MAE (146 against 271 for the GBM). Most policies have no claim, so the median pure premium is 0, and MAE rewards the median. A pricing model that charges nothing would win on MAE.
  • RMSE is nearly identical for all models (about 4,044). It’s dominated by a few huge claims spread over short exposures, which no model can predict.
  • R² is about 0.0005 for both models: practically zero, even though, as the next pages show, both models clearly separate low and high risks.

The problem isn’t the models. These metrics assume errors of similar size everywhere, and for insurance targets that assumption fails badly: most outcomes are exactly zero, the rest vary enormously, and the variance grows with the expected value. Such targets need a loss that matches their distribution: the deviance.

Takeaways

  • Bias checks systematic over- or under-prediction; MAE and RMSE give the typical error size in the target’s unit; R² compares with the Null model.
  • Choosing a metric chooses what the model should predict: RMSE rewards the mean, MAE the median.
  • Plot the residuals. Capped data, non-linear effects and growing error variance stay invisible in summary metrics.
  • For zero-heavy, skewed targets such as pure premium, MAE, RMSE and R² are misleading. Use deviance and ranking-based measures instead.
