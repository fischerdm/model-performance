Error metrics that match the target’s distribution: the right yardstick for insurance targets.

Question answered: how well do the predictions fit, measured in a way that respects the target’s distribution?

The previous page ended with MAE, RMSE and R² failing on pure premium. This page introduces the metric the models were actually trained on: the deviance.

Why squared error doesn’t fit

Squared error treats an error of 100 EUR the same everywhere. That makes sense when every observation is equally noisy. For insurance targets, the noise grows with the expected value: a risk expected to cost 1,000 EUR a year varies far more than one expected to cost 50 EUR. In the Tweedie family of distributions, used for such targets, this is explicit:

\[ \operatorname{Var}(Y) = \phi \, \mu^{p}, \]

where \(\mu\) is the expected value, \(\phi\) a dispersion parameter, and the power \(p\) describes how fast the variance grows with the mean.

Power \(p\) Distribution Typical target
0 Normal errors of constant size (house prices, roughly)
1 Poisson claim counts, claim frequency
1 < p < 2 compound Poisson–Gamma pure premium: exactly 0 often, otherwise positive amounts
2 Gamma claim severity (strictly positive amounts)

Our pure premium GLM uses \(p = 1\.9\).

The deviance

The unit deviance \(d(y, \mu)\) measures how far the prediction \(\mu\) is from the outcome \(y\), on the scale the distribution implies. For the Tweedie family with \(1 < p < 2\):

\[ d(y, \mu) = 2 \left( \frac{y^{2-p}}{(1-p)(2-p)} - \frac{y\,\mu^{1-p}}{1-p} + \frac{\mu^{2-p}}{2-p} \right). \]

It’s twice the log-likelihood gap between a perfect prediction and \(\mu\), so minimizing the mean deviance is maximum likelihood: the same criterion the GLM is fitted with. At \(p = 0\) it reduces to squared error, so squared error is just the deviance for a normal distribution.

Two properties make it suitable for pure premium:

  • Errors are judged relative to the expected level. Missing by 100 EUR on a risk expected to cost 50 EUR counts more than missing by 100 EUR on a risk expected to cost 2,000 EUR.
  • Predictions must be positive. Predicting \(\mu = 0\) for a risk that can have claims gives an infinite deviance. ‘Always 0’, which won on MAE, is ruled out.
Code 
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
# Compound Poisson-Gamma outcomes with mean 200: claims with probability ~5%, average size ~4,000
n_claims = rng.poisson(0.05, 200_000)
outcomes = np.array([rng.gamma(2.0, 2000.0, k).sum() for k in n_claims])
mu = np.linspace(20, 800, 150)
tweedie = np.array([tweedie_deviance(outcomes, m, TWEEDIE_POWER).mean() for m in mu])
squared = np.array([np.mean((outcomes - m) ** 2) for m in mu])

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.8))
for curve, name, color in [(squared, "squared error", METRIC_COLORS[0]),
                           (tweedie, f"Tweedie deviance (p = {TWEEDIE_POWER})", METRIC_COLORS[1])]:
    excess = curve - curve.min()
    scale = excess[np.argmin(np.abs(mu - 100))]
    ax.plot(mu, excess / scale, color=color, label=name)
ax.axvline(outcomes.mean(), color=INK_SECONDARY, linewidth=1)
ax.annotate(f"true mean {outcomes.mean():.0f}", (outcomes.mean(), 1), xycoords=("data", "axes fraction"),
            xytext=(4, -4), textcoords="offset points", va="top", fontsize=9, color=INK_SECONDARY)
ax.set(xlabel="Prediction μ (EUR)", ylabel="Extra loss vs best prediction\n(relative)", ylim=(0, 3))
ax.legend(loc="upper right", bbox_to_anchor=(1, 0.88))

Both losses are minimized at the true mean, so both reward predicting the mean. They differ in how they punish a miss: squared error is symmetric, so predicting 0 costs as much as predicting double the mean. The deviance punishes under-prediction much harder, because a low \(\mu\) claims that large outcomes are nearly impossible.

D²: the deviance version of R²

Like R², the fraction of deviance explained compares a model with the Null model:

\[ D^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i w_i\, d(y_i, \hat\mu_i)}{\sum_i w_i\, d(y_i, \bar y)}. \]

With \(p = 0\) it equals R². Like R², it is 0 for the Null model, can be negative for a model worse than the mean, and can’t be compared across datasets.

pd.DataFrame({
    m: pd.concat([deviance_metrics(y, p[m], p["Null"], w, TWEEDIE_POWER),
                  regression_metrics(y, p[m], w)[["bias", "R²"]]])
    for m in ("Null", "GLM", "GBM")
}).round(4)

Compared with R², the deviance gives a clearer picture:

  • Both models explain about 1.3% of the deviance, roughly 26 times their R² (0.05% for the GBM): R² was drowned out by a few huge claims.
  • The GBM is slightly ahead of the GLM (1.34% against 1.27%). Whether a difference this small is real, and not test-set noise, is a question for the model-comparison page.

Is a D² this small good?

Yes: values like this are normal for individual insurance outcomes. Whether a given policy has a claim this year is mostly chance, and no model can predict chance. A pricing model’s job isn’t to predict which policy will claim but to separate low risks from high risks. That ability shows up clearly in ranking and segmentation tools (Lorenz curves, Gini, lift and double lift charts), which barely react to the noise that dominates individual-level metrics. They are covered on the next page.

Choosing the power

The deviance depends on \(p\), and models can rank differently under different powers. A quick sensitivity check:

Code 
pd.DataFrame({
    f"p = {pw}": {m: deviance_metrics(y, p[m], p["Null"], w, pw)["D²"] for m in ("GLM", "GBM")}
    for pw in (1.2, 1.5, 1.9)
}).round(4)

Here the GBM stays ahead under every power, but the level of D² changes a lot with \(p\). A D² is only meaningful together with the power it was computed with. Pick \(p\) to match the target, ideally the power used to train the model or one estimated from the data, and keep it fixed when comparing models. If the ranking of models flips between plausible powers, neither model is clearly better.

NoteA note on weights

Pure premium is a rate per policy-year, so a policy observed for a week gives a very noisy rate. Weighting each policy’s deviance by its exposure gives a one-week policy 1/52 of the weight of a full-year policy, consistent with how the models were fitted. Unweighted metrics would be dominated by short policies with extreme rates.

Takeaways

  • The deviance is the error measure that matches the target’s distribution, and it’s what GLMs and GBMs actually minimize.
  • For insurance targets use Tweedie (pure premium), Poisson (frequency) or Gamma (severity) deviance, with exposure or claim-count weights.
  • D² is the fraction of the Null model’s deviance explained. Small values are normal when outcomes are mostly chance.
  • When individual outcomes are this noisy, ranking and segmentation measures show a model’s value better than any average error.
