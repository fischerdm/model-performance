Lift charts and Lorenz curves

  • Show All Code

Does the model separate low risks from high risks? Lift charts, double lift charts, Lorenz curves and the Gini index for pure premium.

Question answered: does the model separate low risks from high risks, and is its prediction right for each risk segment?

The deviance page ended with D² values of about 1.3%: individual claims are mostly chance, so any average over individual errors is small. A pricing model is judged on something else: whether the policies it calls expensive really cost more, as a group, than the ones it calls cheap. Grouping averages away the noise. This page covers the standard tools for that:

  • the lift chart: actual against predicted, by decile of the prediction;
  • the double lift chart: two models compared where they disagree;
  • the Lorenz curve and the Gini index: one number for how well a model separates risks.

The lift chart

To draw a lift chart (also called a quantile plot):

  1. sort the test policies by their predicted pure premium,
  2. cut them into 10 groups (deciles) holding the same total exposure,
  3. per decile, compare the exposure-weighted average prediction with the average actual pure premium.

The deciles hold equal exposure rather than equal numbers of policies, because pure premium is a rate per policy-year: a decile full of short policies would hold far less information than its policy count suggests.

Code 
lift = {m: segment_table(y, {m: p[m]}, w, sort_by=p[m]) for m in models}
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.2), sharey=True)
for ax, m in zip(axes, models):
    plot_segments(lift[m], [m], ax=ax, xlabel=f"Decile of {m} prediction (1 = lowest)", title=m)
axes[1].set_ylabel("")
fig.tight_layout()

A good lift chart shows three things:

  • Monotonicity. Actual cost should rise from decile to decile. Both models get there broadly, with a few dips in the middle.
  • Spread. The steeper the actual curve, the better the model separates risks. The ratio of the top to the bottom decile is a common summary (the ‘lift’ that gives the chart its name).
  • Agreement. The predicted line should run through the actual points. A gap shows the predictions are off for that segment even when the overall average is right: calibration, checked by group.
Code 
pd.DataFrame({
    m: {
        "actual, decile 1 (EUR)": t.loc[1, "actual"],
        "actual, decile 10 (EUR)": t.loc[10, "actual"],
        "lift: decile 10 ÷ decile 1": t.loc[10, "actual"] / t.loc[1, "actual"],
        "lift: decile 10 ÷ overall": t.loc[10, "actual"] / mean,
        "predicted ÷ actual, decile 10": t.loc[10, m] / t.loc[10, "actual"],
    }
    for m, t in lift.items()
}).round(2)

The GBM’s top decile costs 7.3 times its bottom decile, against 4.7 times for the GLM. Where the models differ most is the top decile: the GLM predicts 23% more than its riskiest policies actually cost, while the GBM is within 2%. The GLM’s predictions are too extreme at the top, the regression-to-the-mean pattern the calibration page described for probabilities.

NoteRead the intervals before reading the dips

Each decile holds 12,871 to 20,188 policies, but only 312 to 1,496 of them have a claim, and claim sizes vary enormously. The 95% intervals are wide, so a dip such as the GBM’s 4th decile is well within noise. Don’t redesign a model to remove a wiggle that the intervals cover.

The double lift chart

Two lift charts side by side are hard to compare: both look good, and their deciles contain different policies. The double lift chart compares the two models directly, on the policies where they disagree:

  1. put both models on the same overall level (here: rescale each so its average on the test set equals the actual average), so a difference in the overall level doesn’t look like a difference in segmentation;
  2. sort the policies by the ratio GBM ÷ GLM, and cut them into deciles of equal exposure;
  3. per decile, compare the actual average with both models’ averages.

Decile 1 holds the policies the GBM rates much lower than the GLM, decile 10 those it rates much higher. In the middle the models agree.

rebalanced = {m: p[m] * mean / np.average(p[m], weights=w) for m in models}
double = segment_table(y, rebalanced, w, sort_by=rebalanced["GBM"] / rebalanced["GLM"])
Code 
xlabel = "Decile of GBM ÷ GLM (1 = GBM much lower)"
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4.2))
plot_segments(double, models, ax=axes[0], xlabel=xlabel, legend_loc="upper center")
plot_segments(double, models, ax=axes[1], ratio=True, xlabel=xlabel)
fig.tight_layout()

The left panel answers the question ‘who is right where they disagree?’ In decile 1, the GLM predicts 349 EUR, the GBM 182 EUR, and the actual average is 255 EUR. In decile 10, the GLM predicts 104, the GBM 226, and the actual average is 159. Neither model wins: in both tails the truth lies between them.

The right panel shows the same comparison as actual ÷ predicted. The better model is the one whose curve stays flat at 1. Insurers read this as a loss ratio: if premiums were set by the GLM, actual ÷ GLM is the loss ratio each segment would produce.

  • Actual ÷ GLM rises from 0.73 to 1.53. The GBM finds policies the GLM overprices (left) and underprices (right).
  • Actual ÷ GBM falls from 1.40 to 0.71. The GLM finds mispricing in the GBM just as well.

Each model knows something the other doesn’t, and each overreacts where it disagrees with the other. A curve that crosses 1 in the opposite direction would mean the model is worse than useless where the two differ: its deviations from the other model point the wrong way.

What the double lift chart suggests: blend

When both models are partly right, a combination usually beats either. A simple blend is the geometric mean of the two predictions (an average on the log scale, which suits multiplicative pricing models):

blend = np.sqrt(rebalanced["GLM"] * rebalanced["GBM"])
blend *= mean / np.average(blend, weights=w)
Code 
double_blend = segment_table(y, {"Blend": blend, "GBM": rebalanced["GBM"]}, w, sort_by=blend / rebalanced["GBM"])
ax = plot_segments(double_blend, ["Blend", "GBM"], ratio=True, xlabel="Decile of Blend ÷ GBM (1 = Blend much lower)")
ax.figure.set_size_inches(6, 4)
scores = {**{m: p[m] for m in models}, "Blend": blend}
pd.DataFrame({
    m: {"Gini": gini_index(y, s, w), "D²": deviance_metrics(y, s, p["Null"], w, TWEEDIE_POWER)["D²"]}
    for m, s in scores.items()
}).round(4)

Against the blend, the GBM’s actual ÷ predicted swings away from 1 at both ends, while the blend’s stays near 1 within the noise: in this pairing, the blend wins. The blend also has the highest Gini and D². Two caveats: the comparison is on the test set the double lift chart was read from, so a blend chosen this way should be confirmed on fresh data; and whether its improvement is larger than sampling noise is a question for the model-comparison page.

The Lorenz curve and the Gini index

The lift chart shows separation decile by decile. The Lorenz curve shows it as one continuous curve:

  1. sort the policies from the lowest to the highest prediction,
  2. walk along the sorted list and plot the share of total exposure covered so far (x-axis) against the share of total claim cost it contains (y-axis).

A model that can’t separate risks (the Null model) gives the diagonal: the first 30% of exposure carries 30% of the claim cost. A model that separates well puts little claim cost into its low-risk policies, so its curve sags below the diagonal. The oracle sorts by the actual outcome, the best ranking possible.

Code 
ax = plot_lorenz(y, {m: p[m] for m in models}, w)
ax.figure.set_size_inches(5.5, 5.5)

The GBM’s lowest-risk half of the exposure carries only 32% of the claim cost. The Gini index condenses the curve into one number: twice the area between the diagonal and the curve,

\[ \text{Gini} = 1 - 2 \int_0^1 L(x)\,dx, \]

where \(L(x)\) is the share of claim cost in the lowest-predicted share \(x\) of the exposure. It is 0 for the Null model and grows the better the model ranks the policies. The two models score 0.293 (GLM) and 0.294 (GBM), a relative difference of 0.6%. On D² the GBM was 6% ahead, and its top-to-bottom lift was much larger. The Gini weighs the whole ranking, while the lift ratio looks only at the two extreme deciles, each resting on a few hundred claims. A single decile ratio is a much noisier summary than the Gini.

The oracle’s Gini of 0.984 is not a realistic target: 95% of the exposure had no claim at all, and only hindsight can put those policies first. Some authors report a normalized Gini (Gini ÷ oracle Gini), but the oracle mostly reflects how many policies happened to have no claims, so it adds little here.

NoteRelation to the classification Gini

The ROC page defined Gini = 2·AUC − 1 for a binary target. For a 0/1 outcome with equal weights, the Lorenz Gini equals \((1 - \pi)(2 \cdot \text{AUC} - 1)\), where \(\pi\) is the share of positives (the tests in tests/ check this). The factor \(1 - \pi\) is the oracle’s Gini: dividing by it gives the normalized Gini, which equals 2·AUC − 1. Both measure how well the model ranks. The Lorenz version extends the idea to outcomes that aren’t 0/1, such as claim cost, and to weighted observations.

What the Gini can’t see

The Gini depends only on the ranking. Any transformation that keeps the order of the predictions leaves it unchanged, however wrong the predictions become:

squared = p["GBM"] ** 2 * np.average(p["GBM"], weights=w) / np.average(p["GBM"] ** 2, weights=w)
variants = {"GBM": p["GBM"], "2 × GBM": 2 * p["GBM"], "GBM squared": squared}
pd.DataFrame({
    name: {
        "Gini": gini_index(y, s, w),
        "D²": deviance_metrics(y, s, p["Null"], w, TWEEDIE_POWER)["D²"],
        "bias (EUR)": regression_metrics(y, s, w)["bias"],
    }
    for name, s in variants.items()
}).round(4)

Doubling every prediction, or squaring them, keeps the Gini exactly the same. D² drops below 0 (worse than predicting the average for everyone), and a lift chart would show the gap at once. So:

  • Never use the Gini alone. Pair it with a lift chart or a calibration check, and with the deviance.
  • Don’t compare Ginis across portfolios or years. Like AUC, the Gini depends on how heterogeneous the population is: a portfolio with more varied risks gives a higher Gini for an equally good model.
  • Small differences are noise. The GLM and GBM differ by 0.002, far less than the test-set variability of a Gini on data this noisy. Testing such differences is the subject of the model-comparison page.

Which tool for which question

Tool Answers Watch out for
Lift chart Does cost rise with the prediction, and does the prediction match per segment? noisy deciles: read the intervals
Double lift chart Where two models disagree, which is right? rebalance both models to the same level first
Lorenz curve / Gini How well does the model rank risks, in one number? blind to calibration and level; not comparable across portfolios
Deviance / D² How good are the individual predictions, ranking and level together? small values are normal; hard to interpret alone

The tools complement each other: the Gini and the lift spread measure ranking, the agreement in the lift chart measures calibration by segment, the double lift chart compares two candidate models, and the deviance checks both ranking and level at once.

Takeaways

  • With noisy outcomes, judge a pricing model on groups: deciles of equal exposure average away the noise that dominates individual-level metrics.
  • A lift chart should rise monotonically, be steep, and have predictions that run through the actual points. Here the GLM overpredicts its top decile by 23%.
  • A double lift chart compares two models where they disagree. Here each model finds mispricing in the other, and a simple blend beats both.
  • The Gini index summarizes the Lorenz curve: 0.293 (GLM) against 0.294 (GBM). It measures ranking only, so a badly miscalibrated model can have the same Gini as a good one.

as one continuous curve:

1. sort the policies from the **lowest** to the **highest** prediction,
2. walk along the sorted list and plot the **share of total exposure** covered
   so far (x-axis) against the **share of total claim cost** it contains
   (y-axis).

A model that can't separate risks (the Null model) gives the diagonal: the
first 30% of exposure carries 30% of the claim cost. A model that separates
well puts little claim cost into its low-risk policies, so its curve sags
below the diagonal. The **oracle** sorts by the actual outcome, the best
ranking possible.

```{python}
#| label: fig-lorenz
#| fig-cap: "Lorenz curves on the test set, policies sorted from lowest to highest prediction. The dashed curve sorts by the actual outcome."
ax = plot_lorenz(y, {m: p[m] for m in models}, w)
ax.figure.set_size_inches(5.5, 5.5)
```


The GBM's lowest-risk half of the exposure carries only
`{python} f"{half:.0%}"` of the claim cost. The **Gini index** condenses the
curve into one number: twice the area between the diagonal and the curve,

$$
\text{Gini} = 1 - 2 \int_0^1 L(x)\,dx,
$$

where $L(x)$ is the share of claim cost in the lowest-predicted share $x$ of
the exposure. It is 0 for the Null model and grows the better the model ranks
the policies. The two models score `{python} f"{gini['GLM']:.3f}"` (GLM) and
`{python} f"{gini['GBM']:.3f}"` (GBM), a relative difference of
`{python} f"{gini['GBM'] / gini['GLM'] - 1:.1%}"`. On D² the GBM was
`{python} f"{d2['GBM'] / d2['GLM'] - 1:.0%}"` ahead, and its top-to-bottom
lift was much larger. The Gini weighs the whole ranking, while the lift ratio
looks only at the two extreme deciles, each resting on a few hundred claims. A
single decile ratio is a much noisier summary than the Gini.

The oracle's Gini of `{python} f"{oracle:.3f}"` is not a realistic target:
`{python} f"{zero_share:.0%}"` of the exposure had no claim at all, and only
hindsight can put those policies first. Some authors report a **normalized
Gini** (Gini ÷ oracle Gini), but the oracle mostly reflects how many policies
happened to have no claims, so it adds little here.

### What the Gini can't see

The Gini depends **only on the ranking**. Any transformation that keeps the
order of the predictions leaves it unchanged, however wrong the predictions
become:

```{python}

#| tbl-cap: "Three versions of the GBM with identical rankings. 'Squared' is GBM², rescaled to the same average, so its overall level is right but its spread is far too wide."
#| code-fold: false
squared = p["GBM"] ** 2 * np.average(p["GBM"], weights=w) / np.average(p["GBM"] ** 2, weights=w)
variants = {"GBM": p["GBM"], "2 × GBM": 2 * p["GBM"], "GBM squared": squared}
pd.DataFrame({
    name: {
        "Gini": gini_index(y, s, w),
        "D²": deviance_metrics(y, s, p["Null"], w, TWEEDIE_POWER)["D²"],
        "bias (EUR)": regression_metrics(y, s, w)["bias"],
    }
    for name, s in variants.items()
}).round(4)
```

Doubling every prediction, or squaring them, keeps the Gini exactly the same.
D² drops below 0 (worse than predicting the average for everyone), and a lift
chart would show the gap at once. So:

- **Never use the Gini alone.** Pair it with a lift chart or a calibration
  check, and with the deviance.
- **Don't compare Ginis across portfolios or years.** Like AUC, the Gini
  depends on how heterogeneous the population is: a portfolio with more varied
  risks gives a higher Gini for an equally good model.
- **Small differences are noise.** The GLM and GBM differ by
  `{python} f"{gini['GBM'] - gini['GLM']:.3f}"`, far less than the test-set
  variability of a Gini on data this noisy. Testing such differences is the
  subject of the [model-comparison page](../comparison/model-comparison.qmd).

## Which tool for which question

| Tool | Answers | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| Lift chart | Does cost rise with the prediction, and does the prediction match per segment? | noisy deciles: read the intervals |
| Double lift chart | Where two models disagree, which is right? | rebalance both models to the same level first |
| Lorenz curve / Gini | How well does the model rank risks, in one number? | blind to calibration and level; not comparable across portfolios |
| Deviance / D² | How good are the individual predictions, ranking and level together? | small values are normal; hard to interpret alone |

The tools complement each other: the Gini and the lift spread measure
**ranking**, the agreement in the lift chart measures **calibration by
segment**, the double lift chart **compares** two candidate models, and the
deviance checks both ranking and level at once.

## Takeaways

- With noisy outcomes, judge a pricing model on **groups**: deciles of equal
  exposure average away the noise that dominates individual-level metrics.
- A **lift chart** should rise monotonically, be steep, and have predictions
  that run through the actual points. Here the GLM overpredicts its top decile
  by `{python} f"{top_ratio['GLM'] - 1:.0%}"`.
- A **double lift chart** compares two models where they disagree. Here each
  model finds mispricing in the other, and a simple **blend** beats both.
- The **Gini index** summarizes the Lorenz curve: `{python} f"{gini['GLM']:.3f}"`
  (GLM) against `{python} f"{gini['GBM']:.3f}"` (GBM). It measures ranking only,
  so a badly miscalibrated model can have the same Gini as a good one.
