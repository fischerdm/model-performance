French motor insurance (freMTPL2)

The dataset comes from a French insurer and is published on OpenML in two tables: one row per policy (risk features and exposure) and one row per claim (claim amount). It contains 678,013 policies.

Feature Description
Area, Region Where the policyholder lives
Density Inhabitants per km² in the policyholder’s city
DrivAge Age of the driver
BonusMalus Bonus-malus level: below 100 means a claim-free history, above 100 a surcharge
VehPower, VehAge, VehBrand, VehGas Vehicle power, age, brand and fuel
Exposure Fraction of a year the policy was in force (≤ 1)

Targets

HasClaim (binary). 1 if the policy had at least one claim during its exposure period, 0 otherwise. Only 3.7% of policies had a claim, so the classes are heavily imbalanced, which is typical for real classification problems and important for how metrics behave.

PurePremium (continuous). Total claim cost divided by exposure, i.e. the cost per policy-year. It’s what an insurer has to charge (before expenses) to cover claims. 96.3% of policies have a pure premium of 0, and the rest have a long right tail. Because pure premium is a rate per year, a policy observed for a full year carries more information than one observed for a week. All pure-premium models and metrics are therefore weighted by exposure.

NoteData preparation
  • The claim count is taken from the claims table, so HasClaim and PurePremium always agree.
  • Total claim cost per policy is capped at 200,000 EUR (a few claims reach 4 million), and exposure is capped at 1 year.
  • Average cost per claim (‘severity’) was considered as a target and dropped: in this dataset, no model predicts it better than the overall mean.
Code 
pp = policies.loc[policies["PurePremium"] > 0, "PurePremium"]
pd.DataFrame(
    {
        "Statistic": ["Policies", "Claim rate", "Share with pure premium 0",
                      "Median pure premium (when > 0)", "99th percentile (when > 0)"],
        "Value": [n_policies, claim_rate, share_zero,
                  f"{pp.median():,.0f} EUR", f"{pp.quantile(0.99):,.0f} EUR"],
    }
)

Train/test split

Policies are split once into 75% training and 25% test data (169,504 policies), stratified on HasClaim so both parts have the same claim rate. Every metric in this project is computed on the test set. Performance on the training data says how well a model memorized it, not how it will perform on new policies.

California Housing

A classic regression dataset (shipped with scikit-learn): the median house value of 20,640 Californian districts from the 1990 census, in units of 100,000 USD, with 8 features such as median income, house age and location. Unlike pure premium, it has a strong, smooth signal, so we use it to show how error metrics behave in the textbook case. It has one quirk: values were capped at 5.0 when the data was collected, which shows up in the residual plots.

Models

Every target gets the same three models:

Model HasClaim PurePremium California Housing
Null training claim rate training mean (exposure-weighted) training mean
GLM logistic regression Tweedie GLM, power 1.9, log link linear regression
GBM gradient boosting, log loss gradient boosting, Poisson loss gradient boosting, squared error
  • Null is the ‘no model’ reference. Any metric value only means something next to what the Null model achieves.
  • GLM is the traditional, interpretable baseline, still the standard in insurance pricing. The insurance GLMs use one-hot encoded categoricals, binned vehicle and driver age, and log-scaled density and bonus-malus.
  • GBM (HistGradientBoosting in scikit-learn) is the flexible challenger. It handles the categorical features natively and stops adding trees when a validation split stops improving.

The models are deliberately reasonable but not heavily tuned: the project is about measuring performance, not maximizing it.

Code 
b, p, h = binary_predictions(), pure_premium_predictions(), housing_predictions()
rows = {
    "HasClaim (claim rate)": [b["y"].mean()] + [b[m].mean() for m in ("Null", "GLM", "GBM")],
    "PurePremium (EUR/year)": [np.average(p["y"], weights=p["w"])]
        + [np.average(p[m], weights=p["w"]) for m in ("Null", "GLM", "GBM")],
    "House value (100k USD)": [h["y"].mean()] + [h[m].mean() for m in ("Null", "GLM", "GBM")],
}
pd.DataFrame(rows, index=["Actual", "Null", "GLM", "GBM"]).T.round(4)

## French motor insurance (freMTPL2)

The dataset comes from a French insurer and is published on
[OpenML](https://www.openml.org/d/41214) in two tables: one row per policy
(risk features and exposure) and one row per claim (claim amount). It
contains `{python} n_policies` policies.

| Feature | Description |
|---|---|

| `Density` | Inhabitants per km² in the policyholder's city |

| `BonusMalus` | Bonus-malus level: below 100 means a claim-free history, above 100 a surcharge |
| `VehPower`, `VehAge`, `VehBrand`, `VehGas` | Vehicle power, age, brand and fuel |
| `Exposure` | Fraction of a year the policy was in force (≤ 1) |

### Targets

**`HasClaim` (binary).** 1 if the policy had at least one claim during its
exposure period, 0 otherwise. Only `{python} claim_rate` of policies had a

**`PurePremium` (continuous).** Total claim cost divided by exposure, i.e. the
cost per policy-year. It's what an insurer has to charge (before expenses) to
cover claims. `{python} share_zero` of policies have a pure premium of 0, and
the rest have a long right tail. Because pure premium is a rate per year, a
policy observed for a full year carries more information than one observed for
a week. All pure-premium models and metrics are therefore **weighted by
exposure**.
